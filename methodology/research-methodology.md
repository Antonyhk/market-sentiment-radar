# Research Methodology

## 1. Role of sentiment evidence

Sentiment observations are **secondary research inputs**. They may reveal a changing public narrative, crowded interpretation, or topic requiring further work. They do not establish whether a business has a durable moat, whether financial performance will change, or whether an asset is attractively valued.

Every material observation should be linked to a follow-up question that can be examined with higher-quality evidence. Examples include an official company filing, earnings release, investor presentation, operating metric, or independently verifiable event record.

## 2. Source taxonomy

| Source type | Typical content | How it should be used |
|---|---|---|
| `company_disclosure` | Filings, earnings releases, investor presentations, and transcripts | Evidence for factual verification; distinguish reported facts from management guidance. |
| `professional_media` | Attributed reporting from established publications | Context and discovery; retain publication date and original link. |
| `public_social` | Publicly available discussion that may be accessed and used consistently with applicable terms | Surface narratives only; never treat engagement volume as proof. |
| `search_trend` | Aggregated public-search attention data | Context for attention changes, not a measure of investor conviction. |

## 3. Sentiment labels

Use the smallest possible controlled vocabulary:

| Label | Definition |
|---|---|
| `positive` | The observation expresses a favourable view of the named topic or event. |
| `neutral` | The observation is mainly factual or does not establish a directional stance. |
| `negative` | The observation expresses an unfavourable view of the named topic or event. |
| `mixed` | Materially positive and negative views coexist or the direction is ambiguous. |

The label describes the observation, not the likely future performance of a company or security.

## 4. Quality controls

Before treating a narrative as meaningful, check whether it is repeated across independent sources, plausibly linked to an identifiable event, and distinguishable from promotional activity, automated amplification, or reposting. Preserve the original source link and observation date. Do not present unverified social claims as facts.

## 5. Required review questions

1. What exactly changed in the public narrative?
2. What event or evidence might explain the change?
3. Is the observation consistent with the company’s primary disclosures and operating data?
4. What would disconfirm the narrative?
5. What relevant information is absent, stale, or not independently verifiable?

## 6. Data and privacy boundaries

Only record public, necessary, and source-attributed information. Do not collect personal identifiers beyond what is required to cite a public source; do not profile individuals; do not infer sensitive attributes; and do not upload content that violates a source’s terms of use.
